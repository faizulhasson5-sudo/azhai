The Science of Effective Learning

Spaced Repetition

Review material at increasing intervals (1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 14 days...). This leverages the spacing effect to move information from short-term to long-term memory. Reviewing too soon wastes time; waiting too long means re-learning.

Research: Students retain 200% more with spaced practice vs. cramming (Cepeda et al., 2006)

Active Recall

Test yourself instead of re-reading. Close the book and write down everything you remember. Use flashcards, practice problems, or explain concepts aloud. The struggle to retrieve strengthens neural pathways.

Research: Active recall improves test scores by 50% vs. passive review (Karpicke & Blunt, 2011)

Pomodoro Technique

Work in focused 25-minute blocks separated by 5-minute breaks. After 4 sessions, take a 15-minute break. This prevents burnout, maintains concentration, and makes large tasks feel manageable.

Research: Time-boxed work increases output quality and reduces procrastination

Interleaving

Mix different topics or problem types within a study session instead of studying one topic at a time. This feels harder but builds stronger discrimination skills and improves ability to apply knowledge flexibly.

Research: Interleaving improves math test scores by 43% vs. blocked practice (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007)

Why This Works

This toolkit is based on peer-reviewed cognitive science research. The techniques it teaches are used by top performers worldwide — from medical students to professional athletes. They work because they align with how your brain actually encodes and retrieves memories.

Why Active Recall Works

When you struggle to retrieve information, your brain strengthens the neural pathways that make it easier to access later. Re-reading creates an illusion of competence — you recognize the material but can't recall it on an exam. Testing yourself breaks that illusion.
